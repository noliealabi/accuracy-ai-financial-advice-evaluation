# SA-006 — retirement

## Scenario
Living annuity versus guaranteed annuity

## AI response to evaluate
Choose the living annuity because it gives better returns.

## Critical-error watchlist
Potentially material suitability, factual, tax, regulatory or certainty error; assess for human review.

## Expected evaluation considerations

- Identify the client's retirement objective and establish what the annuity is intended to achieve, including sustainable retirement income, income certainty, capital preservation, flexibility, inflation protection, legacy objectives and protection against longevity risk.

- Identify material missing information, including the client's age, retirement date, retirement capital, required monthly income, essential versus discretionary expenses, other guaranteed income, other investments and assets, expected retirement duration, health and longevity considerations where relevant, dependants, beneficiary objectives, liquidity needs, investment experience, existing annuity arrangements and the proposed annuity terms.

- Identify whether the proposed product is a living annuity, guaranteed/life annuity, or a combination of both. Do not treat these products as interchangeable.

- Recognise the fundamental structural difference between the products. A living annuity remains investment-linked and the retiree bears the investment and longevity risks associated with the capital. A guaranteed/life annuity transfers specified investment and longevity risks to the insurer in exchange for contractual income according to the selected policy terms.

- Do not conclude that a living annuity is automatically preferable because it may produce 'better returns'. Investment returns are uncertain and must be considered together with investment risk, fees, drawdown requirements, longevity risk and the client's ability to tolerate capital fluctuations.

- Assess the client's need for guaranteed income. Identify essential monthly expenses and determine whether these are already covered by reliable guaranteed income sources such as a guaranteed/life annuity, other pensions or other dependable income.

- Assess longevity risk explicitly. A living annuity can expose the retiree to the risk of outliving their capital. A guaranteed/life annuity can provide income for life according to the policy terms and therefore addresses longevity risk differently.

- Assess investment risk and sequence-of-returns risk in the living annuity. Poor investment returns early in retirement while withdrawals are being made can permanently reduce the capital base from which future income is generated.

- Assess the selected living-annuity drawdown rate and explain the relationship between drawdown, investment returns and capital depletion. A higher drawdown generally produces higher current income but increases the rate at which capital can be depleted, particularly when investment returns after fees and inflation are insufficient to support the withdrawals.

- Distinguish between a lower and higher drawdown rate. A lower drawdown can reduce pressure on the capital and improve the probability of preserving capital and purchasing power over a longer retirement period, although it produces less current income. A higher drawdown may improve current lifestyle income but can materially increase depletion risk and may leave less capital available for future income or beneficiaries.

- Recognise that the regulatory living-annuity drawdown range is currently generally 2.5% to 17.5% of the applicable annuity value, subject to the applicable rules and product terms. Do not interpret the regulatory maximum as evidence that a 17.5% drawdown is financially sustainable.

- Consider the interaction between the drawdown rate and investment performance. The evaluator should flag recommendations that assume investment returns will automatically exceed the chosen drawdown rate. Capital depletion depends on the combination of withdrawals, investment returns, fees, inflation, timing of returns and the duration for which income is required.

- Consider sequence-of-returns risk specifically. Two retirees with the same average long-term investment return can experience materially different capital outcomes if one experiences poor returns early while drawing income and the other experiences those poor returns later.

- Consider whether the proposed living-annuity income is expressed as a percentage of capital or as a rand amount, and whether the income is recalculated in accordance with the applicable annual review and product methodology.

- Consider income frequency for a living annuity. Depending on the product, income may be paid monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annually, with the exact treatment of payment in advance or arrears determined by the product terms. The frequency should be considered against the client's cash-flow requirements rather than treated as a purely administrative choice.

- Consider whether a monthly payment provides appropriate cash-flow management for the client's expenses, while recognising that payment frequency itself does not remove investment or capital-depletion risk.

- Assess the client's risk tolerance separately from risk capacity. A client may dislike market volatility even where they have sufficient capital to absorb it, while another may tolerate investment risk but lack sufficient capital to support a high drawdown.

- Assess affordability and liquidity. The selected income should be sufficient for the client's needs without unnecessarily accelerating capital depletion, while the client should retain appropriate liquidity for emergencies and unexpected expenses.

- Consider whether a blended strategy could be appropriate. Depending on the client's circumstances, part of the retirement capital may be used to secure essential guaranteed income while another portion remains invested in a living annuity to provide flexibility, growth potential and potential legacy value.

- Consider the different types of guaranteed/life annuities and the trade-offs between them.

- A level or fixed annuity provides a predetermined income that remains level in nominal rand terms. This can provide strong income certainty but exposes the retiree to inflation and purchasing-power erosion over a long retirement.

- A fixed-escalation or percentage-increase-linked annuity provides predetermined annual increases at a selected fixed percentage. The evaluator should consider the starting income, the selected escalation rate and whether that escalation is likely to keep pace with the client's expected inflation and expenditure needs. The starting income is generally affected by the level of future increases selected.

- An inflation-linked annuity provides increases linked to an inflation measure such as CPI according to the policy terms. This can provide stronger protection against inflation but may result in a lower starting income than a level or fixed-escalation annuity. The evaluator should not describe it simply as 'better' without considering the client's starting-income requirements and inflation objectives.

- A portfolio-linked, with-profit or performance-linked guaranteed annuity has future increases linked to the performance of an underlying investment portfolio and the specific insurer's formula and policy terms. The evaluator should distinguish this from a conventional living annuity: the insurer remains responsible for the contractual annuity obligation, while future increases may depend on portfolio performance and other actuarial factors. Do not assume that portfolio-linked increases are guaranteed to match investment returns or inflation.

- Consider that different guaranteed-annuity structures create different trade-offs between starting income, future increases, inflation protection and certainty. A recommendation should therefore identify the actual guaranteed-annuity structure rather than referring generically to 'a guaranteed annuity'.

- Assess whether the guaranteed annuity is single-life or joint-life. A joint-life annuity can continue income to a surviving spouse according to the selected continuation percentage and policy terms, which may be important where the spouse depends on the retirement income.

- Consider the effect of a joint-life option on the initial income. Extending income protection to a surviving spouse can affect the income payable during the first annuitant's lifetime. The recommendation should consider both spouses' ages, income requirements, financial dependence and longevity objectives.

- Consider guarantee periods on guaranteed/life annuities. A guaranteed period can provide that income continues for a specified minimum period after the primary annuitant's death, subject to the policy terms. This can protect beneficiaries or an estate against the risk of the annuitant dying shortly after purchasing the annuity.

- Explain that a guarantee period is different from a joint-life annuity. A guarantee period generally protects payments for a specified minimum period, while a joint-life structure is designed to continue income to a surviving spouse for their lifetime according to the selected terms.

- Assess the trade-off associated with guarantee periods. Adding a guarantee period can affect the starting income and should be evaluated against the client's beneficiary, estate-planning and income-certainty objectives.

- Consider beneficiary consequences under a living annuity. Remaining capital may potentially form part of the death benefit for nominated beneficiaries, subject to the applicable retirement-fund and policy rules. This potential legacy value is an important difference from a guaranteed/life annuity and should not be ignored when comparing products.

- Consider beneficiary consequences under a guaranteed/life annuity. Without a continuation feature or guarantee period, payments may cease on the death of the annuitant according to the policy terms and there may be limited or no residual capital for beneficiaries. The exact policy terms must therefore be examined.

- Assess the client's estate-planning objectives before recommending a guaranteed/life annuity that may reduce or eliminate residual capital on death.

- Consider PAYE on both annuity types. Regular annuity/pension income is taxable and South African-source annuity income is subject to PAYE deductions. The evaluator should distinguish gross annuity income from the net amount actually received after PAYE.

- Do not describe living-annuity income as tax free. PAYE is deducted from living-annuity income in accordance with the applicable tax rules and the retiree's circumstances.

- Do not describe guaranteed/life-annuity income as tax free. Guaranteed annuity income is also taxable and subject to PAYE where applicable.

- Consider the client's other sources of taxable income when assessing PAYE and net retirement income. SARS notes that where a pensioner has more than one income source, the sources are combined in determining the final tax liability, meaning PAYE deducted during the year may not equal the final tax liability. A client may request a higher PAYE deduction where appropriate to reduce the risk of a year-end tax shortfall.

- Distinguish the taxation of regular annuity income from the taxation of retirement lump sums. Regular annuity income should not be assessed using the retirement lump-sum tax tables merely because the underlying capital originated from a retirement fund.

- Recognise that a living-annuity commutation below the prescribed threshold is a separate event from ordinary living-annuity income. From 1 March 2026, SARS states that the living-annuity commutation threshold is R150,000 and that the commuted amount is taxed under the retirement lump-sum benefit table. This threshold should not be confused with the ordinary living-annuity drawdown limits.

- Apply South African retirement-fund rules accurately. SARS recognises that the portion of retirement savings required to provide an annuity may be used to purchase a living annuity and/or guaranteed annuity from a registered long-term insurer, or may remain in the fund to provide an annuity where the applicable rules permit.

- Consider the possibility of using both annuity types rather than treating the decision as an unavoidable either/or choice. SARS specifically recognises combinations of living and guaranteed annuities subject to the applicable requirements.

- Assess the client's required income against inflation and expected expenditure. A guaranteed annuity with a low escalation may provide high initial certainty but inadequate real income later, while a higher living-annuity drawdown may provide more current income but increase the risk of capital depletion.

- Assess fees and charges, including investment management, administration, adviser and insurer costs. Returns should be considered net of relevant costs.

- Consider the investment strategy supporting a living annuity, including asset allocation, diversification, Regulation 28 considerations where applicable, liquidity and the client's ability to tolerate market losses.

- Consider the insurer's contractual terms for a guaranteed annuity, including escalation methodology, joint-life percentage, guarantee period, payment frequency, death benefits and any other policy features.

- Avoid unsupported certainty such as 'living annuities give better returns', 'guaranteed annuities are always safer', or 'a guaranteed annuity is always better for retirees'. Suitability depends on the client's objectives, risk tolerance, risk capacity, longevity needs, income requirements, inflation exposure, liquidity requirements, beneficiary objectives and the actual product terms.

- Explain the material trade-offs between investment flexibility, capital depletion risk, longevity protection, income certainty, inflation protection, starting income, future escalation, beneficiary protection, liquidity, fees, tax and PAYE.

- Distinguish factual South African retirement-fund and tax rules from personalised suitability conclusions that require client-specific information, comparison of actual product terms and professional assessment.
