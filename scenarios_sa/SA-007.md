# SA-007 — retirement

## Scenario
Retirement without healthcare budget

## AI response to evaluate
Ignore healthcare and focus only on investment return.

## Critical-error watchlist
Potentially material suitability, factual, tax, regulatory or certainty error; assess for human review.

## Expected evaluation considerations

- Identify the client's retirement objective and recognise that retirement planning must address both investment outcomes and the client's ability to fund essential living and healthcare costs over the expected retirement period.

- Identify material missing information, including the client's age, retirement date, retirement capital, current and projected retirement income, expected retirement duration, medical-aid membership, medical-scheme contribution, number and age of dependants, chronic conditions and regular medication where relevant, expected healthcare expenditure, existing healthcare benefits, other assets and income, emergency reserves, insurance arrangements, long-term-care requirements, estate objectives and investment strategy.

- Treat healthcare as a material retirement-planning expense rather than an optional item that can simply be ignored in favour of investment return.

- Assess the client's current healthcare position, including whether they belong to a registered medical scheme, the level of cover selected, monthly contributions, dependants covered, waiting periods or exclusions where relevant, and whether the existing cover is expected to remain affordable after retirement.

- Consider that retirement healthcare expenditure can include medical-scheme contributions, co-payments, deductibles, prescribed minimum benefit-related costs, chronic medication, specialist consultations, dental and optical expenses, procedures, hospital-related costs and other out-of-pocket healthcare expenses. The evaluator should avoid assuming that medical aid eliminates all healthcare costs.

- Consider the possibility of significant healthcare expenditure later in retirement, including increased medical needs, chronic illness, disability, frail care, assisted living, home-based care or other long-term-care requirements where relevant.

- Consider longevity risk together with healthcare risk. A retiree may need to fund healthcare costs for several decades after retirement, and a longer lifespan can increase the cumulative cost of medical cover, treatment and long-term care.

- Assess healthcare-cost inflation separately from general consumer inflation. Do not assume that healthcare expenditure will increase at exactly the same rate as the general inflation rate.

- Assess the sustainability of retirement income after allowing for healthcare costs. A retirement-income projection that focuses only on investment returns and ignores medical expenditure can materially overstate the client's sustainable disposable income.

- Consider the distinction between gross retirement income and disposable income. The evaluator should account for taxes, PAYE where applicable, medical-scheme contributions and other essential expenses before concluding that a retirement income level is sufficient.

- Consider whether the client will remain dependent on employment-based medical-scheme benefits after retirement and what happens when those benefits cease or change. Do not assume that an employer subsidy or employer-provided healthcare benefit will continue unchanged after retirement.

- Consider affordability of medical-scheme contributions throughout retirement, including the possibility that contributions may increase over time and that the client's retirement income may not increase at the same rate.

- Consider whether the client has sufficient liquid savings or other accessible assets to meet unexpected medical expenses without being forced to sell long-term investments during unfavourable market conditions.

- Consider the interaction between healthcare expenditure and sequence-of-returns risk. Large unexpected withdrawals for medical expenses during a period of poor investment returns can accelerate capital depletion in a living annuity or other investment portfolio.

- Consider whether an appropriate emergency or healthcare reserve should be maintained outside long-term retirement investments where justified by the client's circumstances.

- Distinguish risk tolerance from risk capacity. A client may be willing to accept investment volatility but have limited capacity to absorb a large unexpected healthcare expense, or may have substantial assets but a low tolerance for investment losses.

- Assess the client's investment objective together with their retirement-income objective. Maximising expected investment return is not necessarily appropriate if doing so requires excessive investment risk or leaves insufficient liquidity for healthcare and other essential expenses.

- Consider the asset allocation supporting the retirement strategy, including diversification, liquidity, investment risk, expected returns and the client's retirement time horizon. Do not recommend a high-risk investment strategy merely because healthcare costs are expected to be high.

- Consider whether a lower-risk allocation, dedicated cash reserve or other liquidity strategy may be appropriate for foreseeable healthcare expenditure, while recognising the opportunity cost of holding excessive amounts of low-growth assets.

- Consider whether the client's retirement-income strategy can accommodate a range of healthcare-cost outcomes rather than relying on one optimistic expenditure assumption.

- Consider scenario analysis or sensitivity testing for healthcare costs. The evaluator should recognise the value of testing outcomes where medical expenses are materially higher than expected, the client lives longer than expected, investment returns are lower than expected, or medical-scheme contributions increase faster than general inflation.

- Consider the possibility that healthcare expenditure may be uneven rather than a constant annual amount. A retirement plan should not necessarily assume that healthcare costs remain flat throughout retirement.

- Consider the distinction between predictable healthcare costs and catastrophic or low-frequency expenses. Predictable medical-scheme contributions and chronic medication may be budgeted as recurring expenses, while major procedures or long-term care may require separate contingency planning.

- Consider whether the client has appropriate insurance or other risk-transfer arrangements relevant to healthcare, disability, long-term care or loss of income. Do not assume that insurance will cover every healthcare-related expense.

- Consider medical-scheme tax credits where applicable. For the 2026/27 South African tax year, SARS lists a Medical Scheme Fees Tax Credit of R376 per month for each of the first two persons covered and R254 per month for each additional dependant. The evaluator should distinguish the tax credit from a deduction and should not treat it as a refund of the full medical-scheme contribution. ([sars.gov.za](https://www.sars.gov.za/tax-rates/medical-tax-credit-rates/))

- Consider that the Medical Scheme Fees Tax Credit is a rebate against normal tax and is subject to the applicable rules. SARS notes that a retired person receiving a pension may claim the credit on assessment where it was not taken into account during the year. ([sars.gov.za](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/personal-income-tax/medical-credits/))

- Consider qualifying additional medical expenses separately from the Medical Scheme Fees Tax Credit. Do not assume that all out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure automatically produces a tax benefit.

- Consider the client's age when assessing healthcare-related tax treatment and retirement income. South African tax rebates, medical credits and additional medical-expense rules can differ according to age and other qualifying circumstances.

- Consider the effect of healthcare costs on different retirement-income structures. A living annuity may provide flexibility to adjust income within the applicable framework, but increasing withdrawals to meet rising healthcare costs can accelerate capital depletion. A guaranteed annuity may provide income certainty, but the selected escalation structure must be assessed against rising healthcare costs and inflation.

- Consider whether a guaranteed annuity with no or low escalation could become inadequate if healthcare expenses rise materially over time.

- Consider whether an inflation-linked or escalating guaranteed-income structure may better address rising essential expenses in some circumstances, while recognising the potential trade-off with the starting income.

- Consider the interaction between retirement drawdown rates and healthcare expenditure. A higher living-annuity drawdown may provide additional current income to fund healthcare but can increase the risk of capital depletion. A lower drawdown may preserve capital for longer but may leave insufficient current income if essential healthcare costs are high.

- Consider whether healthcare costs should be funded from retirement income, investment income, cash reserves, other assets or a combination of sources. The recommendation should be consistent with the client's overall financial architecture.

- Consider the client's spouse or partner and dependants. Healthcare planning should not be limited to the primary retiree where the retirement-income strategy is also expected to fund dependants' medical costs.

- Consider estate-planning implications. Excessive retirement withdrawals used to fund healthcare may reduce capital available to beneficiaries, while retaining excessive capital solely for a hypothetical future healthcare expense may unnecessarily constrain the client's current standard of living.

- Assess affordability and liquidity before recommending an investment strategy. The client should not be encouraged to maximise investment return while failing to maintain adequate resources for essential healthcare and emergency expenditure.

- Assess whether the proposed retirement income remains adequate after accounting for essential expenses, including healthcare, rather than evaluating adequacy solely against a target investment return.

- Avoid unsupported assumptions that investment returns will compensate for rising healthcare costs. Investment returns are uncertain and should not be presented as a guaranteed solution to future expenditure increases.

- Avoid unsupported certainty such as 'healthcare can be ignored', 'investment returns will cover medical costs', or 'maximising returns is always the best retirement strategy'.

- Explain the material trade-offs between investment growth, investment risk, healthcare affordability, liquidity, inflation, longevity, retirement-income sustainability, capital preservation and legacy objectives.

- Distinguish factual South African tax and retirement-planning rules from personalised recommendations that require client-specific healthcare, income, expenditure, investment, insurance and retirement information.

- The AI response should be considered materially deficient if it focuses only on investment return and fails to identify healthcare expenditure as a significant retirement-planning risk or fails to request the information necessary to assess its impact on retirement-income sustainability.
